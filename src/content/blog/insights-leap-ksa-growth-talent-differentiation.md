By [Yulia Porozova](https://www.linkedin.com/in/yulia-porozova-qatar/), Account and Business Development Manager, Chesamel

When I walked into [LEAP](https://onegiantleap.com/?utm_source=google&utm_medium=organic_search&utm_campaign=Unspecified) KSA, the first thing that hit me was the energy.

The scale was enormous. Thousands of people were moving between stands, new technologies were being demonstrated in every direction, and everyone seemed ready to meet, connect and explore the next opportunity.

But as I spent time at the event, I found myself thinking about what all this activity tells us about where the Saudi market is heading.

Over several days, I spoke with exhibitors, technology providers and growing businesses. Two challenges came through particularly clearly: keeping good people and standing out in a market where so many companies seem to be promising something similar.

#### **Technology is moving quickly and the competition is getting louder**

Innovation naturally dominated the show floor. Data storage and cybersecurity were especially visible, with organisations responding to growing demand for secure, in-country data storage, particularly among government and public-sector clients.

I came across specialist providers focused on areas such as data storage and security, alongside companies offering broader, end-to-end digital transformation services. There certainly wasn't a shortage of capability.

What I sometimes found harder was understanding the difference between one provider and the next.

Many businesses described themselves in much the same way: end-to-end, innovative, highly experienced and powered by the best people. These are reasonable messages, but when dozens of companies make the same claims, they stop helping buyers choose.

For me, “digital transformation” is starting to carry the same problem. It covers so much that, without a specific outcome attached to it, it can mean very little.

The technology might be impressive. But if customers can't quickly understand what makes it different or how it will make their working lives better, the message gets lost in the crowd.

#### **Strong technology needs a clear story**

One of my clearest takeaways was how much attention businesses were giving to their products and technical capabilities, compared with how they communicated their value.

That creates a real commercial problem. In a crowded market, buyers don't have time to decode complicated service descriptions or work out why one broad transformation offer is better than another. They need to understand the problem being solved, the outcome they can expect and why a particular business is equipped to deliver it.

I see this as a question of making the value easier to understand while keeping the technical substance. For businesses entering or growing within Saudi Arabia, I'd start with a few straightforward questions:

- What do we want to be known for?
- Which client problem do we solve particularly well?
- Who feels that problem most urgently?
- How do we make their work, operations or business performance better?
- What evidence shows that we can deliver?

Trying to be known for everything makes it harder to be remembered for something specific. A clearer focus, a sharper point of view and real proof of delivery will do far more than another promise of “end-to-end digitalisation”.

#### **Keeping good people was a recurring concern**

Another theme came up repeatedly in my conversations: businesses were worried about keeping the people they had recruited and trained.

I heard concerns about employees moving quickly when a better opportunity appeared, alongside changing expectations around flexibility, progression and work-life balance, particularly among younger professionals.

For employers, that can become a costly cycle. They recruit someone, invest time and money in training them, and then lose that knowledge just as the person becomes fully effective.

My takeaway was that businesses need to look beyond the next hire. They need to understand why people stay, what helps them perform and where permanent recruitment is the right answer. They also need a workforce model that keeps delivery moving when somebody leaves.

That might mean improving the employee experience and creating clearer opportunities for development. It could also mean bringing in embedded specialists to add capacity, transfer knowledge or support priority programmes without placing every responsibility on the permanent team.

For me, the objective is to strengthen internal teams and build a more resilient workforce around them.

#### **Saudi Arabia is becoming a base for regional growth**

One of the most striking shifts I noticed was the number of multinational companies looking at Saudi Arabia as a regional base.

For years, the UAE was widely treated as the default location for regional headquarters. My conversations at LEAP suggested that more businesses are now considering Saudi Arabia as a place from which to lead their regional growth.

That creates opportunities, and it raises the standard for companies hoping to compete. Entering the market without a clear position, the right people or a realistic delivery plan makes it much harder to turn momentum into sustainable growth.

My advice is simple: start now, and plan well. The momentum I saw was real. Have the right people around you and be specific about what you want to be known for.

#### **Turning ambition into something customers can recognise**

The businesses I met at LEAP weren't short of ambition. They were building, expanding and looking for the next opportunity.

What stayed with me was the challenge of turning that ambition into something customers and employees can recognise in their day-to-day experience.

For customers, that means a clear proposition and a compelling reason to choose your business. For employees, it means an environment in which they can grow, contribute and see a future. Leadership teams need access to the right capabilities to support both, without progress slowing every time the organisation needs to recruit, restructure or enter a new market.
