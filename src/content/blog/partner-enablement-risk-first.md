Somewhere today, a partner is describing your product to a buyer you will never meet. The question that should keep commercial leaders awake is simple: are they describing the product you actually sell?

The evidence says probably not. Gartner’s survey of 632 B2B buyers found that 69% report inconsistencies ([gartner.com](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-sales-survey-finds-61-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-buying-experience)) between the information on a sales organisation’s website and what they hear from sellers. That figure covers your own people. Now extend it to partners, who receive a fraction of the training, and the exposure becomes obvious. The same research found 73% of buyers actively avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach ([gartner.com](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-sales-survey-finds-61-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-buying-experience)). When a partner gets your story wrong, the buyer does not blame the partner. They quietly remove you from the shortlist.

### **Enablement is a control system, and most firms run it as a library**

The standard model treats partner enablement as voluntary learning: a portal, a certification, a quarterly webinar with 12% attendance. This assumes partners will invest their own time to protect your brand. They will not, and it is unreasonable to expect them to.

A risk-first model starts from a different question: at which moments of execution is a partner most likely to break trust in our name? Typically it is four moments. First contact, where positioning gets improvised. Scoping, where capabilities get overpromised. Pricing conversations, where discounting habits leak. And post-sale handover, where accountability goes quiet.

Enablement should be built backwards from those four failure points, delivered inside the partner’s actual workflow, at the moment of execution, with the shortest possible path between question and correct answer.

### **Unpopular opinion**

Your best partners are grading you on utility, whatever the partnership deck says. The partners who rave about your “partnership culture” are usually the ones producing the least pipeline. High performers judge you on one thing: whether working with you makes their next deal faster and safer than working with your competitor. Build for utility and the relationship follows. Build for the relationship and you get lunches.

### **The Partner Risk Scorecard**

Score each active partner from 1 (low risk) to 5 (high risk) across five dimensions:

1. **Message drift**: how far does their pitch deviate from your current positioning?
2. **Scope accuracy**: how often do their proposals promise things you cannot deliver?
3. **Commercial discipline**: do they protect your pricing integrity or erode it?
4. **Escalation behaviour**: do problems reach you early, late, or via the customer?
5. **Content currency**: are they selling from this quarter’s material or 2024’s?

Any partner scoring 15 or above is an active brand liability, whatever their revenue number says. Revenue tells you what a partner produced. The scorecard tells you what they might cost you.

### **What to do this quarter**

Run the scorecard across your top twenty partners and rank by risk, ignoring revenue for the exercise. Rebuild your enablement calendar around the four failure moments rather than around your product release schedule. Kill any asset a partner cannot use mid-conversation. And put one number in front of your leadership monthly: the percentage of partner-led deals where the buyer heard a current, accurate version of your story.

Partners amplify whatever you give them. Give them ambiguity and they will scale it.
